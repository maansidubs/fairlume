The scale runs 0 to 100 and its midpoint is 50. Because training was rebalanced to stop the rarer outcome being ignored, this is a position on that scale rather than a calibrated chance of default.

    A number on its own is not allowed to be the answer.

    When a lender in the United States turns someone down, it owes them more than the outcome. Under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act the applicant is entitled to the specific principal reasons behind the decision. Not a score band. Not a risk tier. Reasons.

    That requirement decides the shape of everything downstream of it. A model returning a probability and nothing else cannot be put in front of a customer no matter how well it performs, because there is no lawful way to act on what it says. The explanation is not the presentation layer sitting on top of the real work. It is part of the specification.

    The score and the reasons leave Fairlume in the same response, from the same fitted model, every time it is called.

    One and a third million loans, each with its ending.

    The data is the complete public file of a marketplace lender: every application it funded, the terms it granted, and whether the borrower eventually repaid. After cleaning, 1.37 million loans remain with a settled outcome, and 22.4% of them ended in default or charge off.

    Records like this are rarer than they ought to be. This one exists because retail investors were funding individual loans and needed the underwriting file to choose between them. When the platform wound that business down and reorganised itself as a bank, the reason to publish went with it, and nothing of comparable depth has appeared since. Banks hold far better data and are under no obligation to release any of it.

    Validation is chronological rather than random. The model is fitted on one stretch of lending and graded on the stretch that follows, which is the only arrangement that matches how the thing would be used. A random split lets a model study the same period it is later tested on, and quietly flatters it.

    What the model is actually doing.

    The question is binary and narrow: given what was known at the moment of application, does this loan end in default. Loans still being repaid are excluded rather than counted as good, because their ending has not happened yet and treating an unfinished loan as a success teaches the model that patience is safety.

    Fifteen inputs feed it, all of them facts an applicant could state or a bureau could confirm before any decision is made. Nothing that only becomes knowable afterwards is allowed in, which sounds obvious and is the most common way a credit model quietly cheats.

    Because roughly one loan in four defaults, the training objective is reweighted so the rarer outcome is not simply optimised away. Without that, the cheapest strategy available to any classifier is to predict that nobody ever defaults, and it will find it.

    Logistic regression came first. Not ceremony, but a floor: the gradient boosted model has to beat a linear one by enough to be worth the loss of transparency, and if it cannot, the linear one ships. XGBoost clears it, and the margin is stated below rather than dressed up.

    Attribution uses SHAP rather than the importance chart XGBoost provides for free. That built in chart ranks features by how much they helped the trees split during training, which is a statement about the fitting process, not about any individual borrower. Ranked the second way instead, the weight moves noticeably: the term of the loan falls back and revolving utilisation, debt to income and recent inquiry activity come forward. For a model whose entire job is telling one person why, only the per applicant measure is admissible.

    Read plainly: take one borrower who defaulted and one who repaid, at random. The model puts them in the right order about seventy one times in a hundred. Among the applicants it flags as risky, defaults are concentrated roughly forty percent more densely than chance would manage.

    Accuracy is left out on purpose. A model predicting that nobody will ever default scores 77.6% here and catches none of them, which is why the figure appears in slide decks and not in credit teams.

    These numbers are moderate, and that is the honest ceiling rather than a shortfall. Most consumer default is caused by events that appear nowhere in an application: a job lost, an illness, a separation, a car that finally dies. A model scoring 0.95 on this problem would not be brilliant. It would be reading something it should not have been given.

    What a lender deploys is never a probability. It is a threshold, and the threshold is a commercial decision.

    Move the cutoff and the same model becomes cautious or permissive without a line of retraining. Where it lands depends on arithmetic outside the model entirely: a default costs the principal, a good applicant wrongly declined costs the margin they would have paid, and the ratio between those two sets the line. The model's job is to order the queue accurately and account for the order it chose. Everything after that belongs to the business.

    What it cannot do.

    It has never seen a downturn

    The lending it learned from happened in calm conditions. How default behaviour shifts under a real macroeconomic shock is outside its experience entirely, and none of the figures above would survive one intact.

    The population is not the population

    Everyone in this file already cleared the platform's own filter. The applicants turned away before that point are invisible here, so the model learns from survivors and inherits that selection without ever being told about it.

    Fairness is untested

    Disparate impact across protected groups has not been measured. Leaving protected attributes out of the file does not remove the problem, since ordinary variables can stand in for them, and the test that would settle it is unfinished work.

    Attribution is not causation

    These factors describe what the model weighted. They do not predict what would happen if a real person changed one of them, and reading them as advice would be a misuse of the output.
